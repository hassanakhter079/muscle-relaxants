Classification

Two Completely Different Drug Families

"Muscle relaxant" is an umbrella covering two mechanistically unrelated groups. Confusing them is a dangerous clinical error.

⚡
Neuromuscular Blocking Agents (NMBAs) — block the NMJ. Cause complete flaccid paralysis. Patient cannot breathe. Exclusively used in anaesthesia/ICU with mandatory airway control.

Spasmolytics — act centrally or peripherally to reduce muscle spasm/spasticity. Patient stays conscious and breathes independently. Used in neurology, orthopaedics, pain medicine.
Neuromuscular Blockers — Type I

Depolarising NMBs

Mimic ACh at the NMJ but resist hydrolysis → sustained depolarisation → muscle inexcitable (Phase I block). Only one clinical agent remains.

Suxamethonium
Succinylcholine · SCh
Depolarising
Dose IV: 1–1.5 mg/kg (RSI) / max 150 mg
Paediatric: 2 mg/kg IV (infant) · 1–1.5 mg/kg child
IM: 3–4 mg/kg (if no IV access) / delayed onset
Onset 30–60 sec IV
Duration 8–12 min (hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase)
Use
RSI Laryngospasm Emergency intubation Electroconvulsive therapy
MOA: Binds nicotinic ACh receptors (α-subunits) at NMJ → sustained depolarisation (fasciculations) → receptor desensitisation → muscle inexcitable (Phase I block). Structurally two ACh molecules joined: resistant to acetylcholinesterase but hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase).
CI: Burns >24h, denervation injury, crush injury, prolonged immobility, hyperkalaemia, malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, pseudocholinesterase deficiency, penetrating eye injury, myopathies, personal/family Hx of MH
Special Phase II block (dual block) occurs at high/repeated doses — mimics non-depolarising block. No antidote; neostigmine worsens Phase I block.
Decamethonium
Obsolete depolarising agent
Depolarising
Status No longer in clinical use. Not hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase — prolonged action made it impractical.
MOA: Same as suxamethonium — NMJ depolarisation. Mentioned only for completeness in pharmacology exams.
Neuromuscular Blockers — Type II

Non-Depolarising NMBs

Competitive antagonists at nicotinic ACh receptors. No fasciculations. Reversible with anticholinesterases (or sugammadex for amino-steroids).

Rocuronium
Aminosteroidal
Non-Depol

Maintenance: 0.1–0.2 mg/kg IV bolus
Infusion: 0.3–0.6 mg/kg/hr
Onset 60–90 sec (dose-dependent)
Duration 30–60 min (intermediate)
Use
RSI (alt to SCh) Elective intubation ICU paralysis Surgical relaxation
MOA: Competitive antagonism at α-subunit of nicotinic ACh receptor at NMJ. No channel activation → no depolarisation → flaccid paralysis. Reversed by sugammadex (encapsulates rocuronium) or neostigmine (inhibits AChE → more ACh competes).
Excretion Primarily hepatic/biliary. Use with caution in liver disease.

Spasmolytics — CNS-Acting

Central Muscle Relaxants

Reduce muscle tone via CNS pathways — spinal cord interneurons, α2-receptors, or GABA-B receptors. No NMJ action. Patient remains conscious.

Baclofen
GABA-B agonist
Spasmolytic
Dose Oral: Start 5 mg TDS → titrate to 20–80 mg/day in divided doses
Intrathecal: 25–1200 mcg/day via pump (severe spasticity)
Use
MS spasticity SCI Cerebral palsy Trigeminal neuralgia Hiccups (off-label)
MOA: GABA-B receptor agonist (Gi-coupled) at presynaptic terminals in spinal cord dorsal horn → ↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ glutamate/aspartate release from afferent fibres + postsynaptic hyperpolarisation → reduced polysynaptic reflex arc → ↓ muscle tone.
Abrupt withdrawal → severe rebound: hyperthermia, seizures, rhabdomyolysis (life-threatening). Taper slowly. Intrathecal pump failure = medical emergency.
Kinetics Absorbed orally. Renal excretion 70–80% unchanged. Dose reduce in renal failure. Does not cross BBB well — explains high intrathecal doses.
Tizanidine
α2-adrenergic agonist
Spasmolytic
Dose Oral: 2–4 mg TDS (max 36 mg/day)
Start 2 mg nocte, titrate over weeks
Use
Spasticity (MS, SCI) Muscle spasm — back pain Neck pain
MOA: Presynaptic α2-adrenergic agonist in spinal cord → inhibits excitatory interneurons → ↓ release of excitatory amino acids (glutamate, aspartate) from Ia afferents → reduces polysynaptic motor neuron facilitation. Similar mechanism to clonidine.
Kinetics Short half-life ~2.5 h. Hepatic metabolism via CYP1A2. Significant food-dose interaction — fed state alters peak and AUC.
Hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs). Sedation, hypotension. Avoid with ciprofloxacin/fluvoxamine (CYP1A2 inhibitors → toxic plasma levels). Avoid in hepatic impairment.
Cyclobenzaprine
TCA-related centrally-acting
Spasmolytic
Dose Standard: 5–10 mg TDS (max 30 mg/day, max 3 weeks)
Elderly: 5 mg TDS (avoid 10 mg)
Use
Acute musculoskeletal spasm Low back pain (short-term) Neck pain
MOA: Structurally similar to TCAs (dibenzazepine nucleus). Acts on brainstem → reduces tonic somatic motor activity by inhibiting descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways. Anticholinergic + antihistaminic effects contribute to sedation.
CI in acute recovery phase post-MI, arrhythmias, heart block, hyperthyroidism. MAOi interaction (serotonin syndrome risk). Glaucoma, urinary retention (anticholinergic). Not for spasticity (MS/SCI) — only musculoskeletal spasm.

Meprobamate
Propanediol carbamate

High overdose lethality. Controlled substance. Largely not recommended in modern practice.
Peripherally Acting

Dantrolene Sodium

The only clinically used peripherally acting skeletal muscle relaxant. Unique and irreplaceable mechanism.

Dantrolene
Hydantoin-class nitrophenyl-furanyl derivative
Peripheral
Dose Malignant Hyperthermia (emergency):
2.5 mg/kg IV bolus → repeat q5–10 min PRN → total up to 10 mg/kg

MH Prophylaxis (oral, pre-op):
4–8 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses × 1–2 days pre-op (rarely used now)

Spasticity (oral):
Start 25 mg/day → titrate to 100 mg QDS (max 400 mg/day)

NMS treatment:
1–2.5 mg/kg IV q6h
Use
Malignant hyperthermia — treatment Neuroleptic malignant syndrome Serotonin syndrome (adjunct) Spasticity (MS, CP, SCI) Exertional heat stroke
MOA: Directly inhibits ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1) on the sarcoplasmic reticulum of skeletal muscle → ↓ Ca²⁺ release from SR → ↓ actin-myosin cross-bridge formation → muscle relaxation. Acts WITHIN the muscle cell — not at NMJ, not at CNS. Only agent targeting the E-C coupling step directly.

In MH: volatile anaesthetics/SCh trigger uncontrolled RyR1 opening → massive Ca²⁺ release → hyperthermia, rigidity, acidosis. Dantrolene blocks this by stabilising RyR1.
Hepatotoxicity (fatal cases with oral long-term use — monitor LFTs every 3 months). Severe muscle weakness. Respiratory muscle involvement possible. Do not use in chronic obstructive pulmonary or cardiac conditions at spastic doses. IV formulation: older preparation (Dantrium) requires mannitol as vehicle.
Adjuncts with Muscle Relaxant Properties

Benzodiazepines + Clonidine + Botulinum

Not primary muscle relaxants, but widely used for muscle spasm, spasticity, or procedural muscle relaxation.

Diazepam
Benzodiazepine
BZD
Dose Muscle spasm: 2–10 mg TDS/QDS oral
Spasticity: 2–10 mg TDS
Tetanus adjunct: 5–10 mg IV q4–6h; ICU: 100–300 mg/day infusion
Acute back spasm: 5–10 mg TDS × 5–7 days
Use
Tetanus Acute muscle spasm Alcohol withdrawal spasm Cerebral palsy spasticity Procedural muscle relaxation
MOA: Positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A receptor (benzodiazepine site) → ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening → neuronal hyperpolarisation → CNS depression + ↓ spinal cord interneuron excitability → muscle relaxation. Also ↓ anxiety component of muscle guarding.
Dependence/tolerance. Respiratory depression especially with opioids (FDA black box). Not for long-term spasticity if CNS depression would worsen patient function. Long half-life (20–100h) + active metabolites (desmethyldiazepam) — accumulates in elderly/hepatic impairment.

Also a potent anticonvulsant.
Botulinum Toxin A/B

Spread to adjacent muscles. Risk of dysphagia/aspiration if near swallowing muscles. Systemic botulism (extremely rare — dose-dependent). Contraindicated in NMJ disorders (MG, Eaton-Lambert). Antibody formation reduces efficacy over time (especially Type B).

Clinical Decision Framework

Which Drug for Which Situation?

Scenario-based guide — first choice and rationale.

🚨
Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI)
Suxamethonium 1.5 mg/kg Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg (alt)

SCh: fastest onset, ultra-short duration — ideal for failed airway (spontaneous recovery). Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg matches SCh onset but lasts ~70 min — only safe if sugammadex (16 mg/kg) immediately available for reversal in cannot-intubate-cannot-oxygenate scenario.

🫁
ARDS / Prolonged ICU Ventilation
Cisatracurium infusion

Organ-independent elimination (Hofmann) — no dose adjustment in renal/hepatic failure. Minimal histamine. Lower laudanosine than atracurium. ACURASYS trial evidence for early paralysis in ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ <150). Note: ROSE trial didn't show mortality benefit at 48h — use selectively.

🔥
Malignant Hyperthermia
Dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV — URGENT

ONLY treatment. Give immediately — do not delay for confirmation. Stop volatile agent + SCh. Continue repeating q5–10 min until rigidity/hyperthermia resolves (up to 10 mg/kg total). Maintain dantrolene 1 mg/kg IV q4–6h × 24–48h post-crisis. MH Hotline (USA): 1-800-MH-HYPER.

🧠
MS / SCI Spasticity
Baclofen (oral → intrathecal) Tizanidine (alt)

Baclofen is first-line for spasticity of spinal origin. Intrathecal baclofen pump for severe cases refractory to oral. Tizanidine is second-line — monitor hepatotoxicity. Avoid benzodiazepines long-term (dependence, cognitive dulling). Botulinum toxin for focal spasticity (e.g., spastic equinus, elbow flexors).

🦴
Acute Musculoskeletal Spasm (Back/Neck)
Cyclobenzaprine 5–10 mg TDS Diazepam 5 mg TDS (alt) Methocarbamol (alt)

Short-term only (≤2–3 weeks). Cyclobenzaprine has most evidence. Add NSAIDs + physiotherapy. Avoid opioids as first-line. Tizanidine is an option. Benzodiazepines effective but addiction risk limits use.

⚡
Tetanus
Diazepam (high dose) Vecuronium/Pancuronium (severe) Methocarbamol (mild)

Mild–moderate: diazepam 5–10 mg IV q4–6h. Severe (intubated ICU): add IV non-depolarising NMB (vecuronium preferred — no vagolytic) for complete motor control. High-dose IV diazepam infusion (100–300 mg/day) historically used. Baclofen intrathecal emerging evidence. Avoid SCh for intubation in generalised tetanus (hyperkalaemia risk).

🧪
Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)
Dantrolene 1–2.5 mg/kg IV Bromocriptine 2.5 mg TDS (D2 agonist)

Stop offending antipsychotic. Cooling, hydration, ICU. Dantrolene reduces hyperthermia and rigidity via RyR1 block. Bromocriptine restores dopaminergic tone. Diazepam for agitation. Distinguish from SS: NMS slower onset, lead-pipe rigidity, no hyperreflexia/clonus.

💉
Focal Spasticity (Stroke / CP)
Botulinum Toxin A

Most effective for focal spasticity — targeted chemodenervation without systemic CNS depression. Functional goal setting essential. Combined with physiotherapy/casting. Retreatment every 3–6 months. Systemic relaxants unnecessary if spasticity is limited to one limb.

❤️
Cardiac Surgery / CVS-Sensitive Patient
Vecuronium Cisatracurium

Vecuronium: no histamine, no vagolytic, no ganglion block → haemodynamically neutral. Avoid pancuronium (tachycardia/hypertension) and atracurium (histamine release/hypotension). Cisatracurium also acceptable — organ-independent.

🫘
Renal + Hepatic Failure
Cisatracurium or Atracurium

Hofmann elimination makes these organ-independent. Atracurium releases histamine. Cisatracurium preferred. Avoid vecuronium (active metabolite in renal failure), rocuronium (hepatic), pancuronium (renal excretion).

🏃
Short Elective Procedure
Mivacurium 0.2 mg/kg Suxamethonium (if no CI)

Mivacurium: shortest non-depol duration (~15 min). Ideal where spontaneous recovery preferred and extended paralysis unnecessary. Check pseudocholinesterase history before use.

🍺
Alcohol Withdrawal with Tremor/Spasm
Diazepam or Lorazepam

Benzodiazepines are first-line for alcohol withdrawal (seizure prevention + muscle spasm). CIWA protocol guided dosing. Baclofen has emerging evidence in alcohol use disorder reduction but not standard for acute withdrawal spasm.

Safety Profile

Adverse Effects — Clinically Important

Key adverse effects per agent — particularly the ones that cause patient harm.

Suxamethonium
  • Malignant Hyperthermia trigger
  • Hyperkalaemia → cardiac arrest (burns, SCI, denervation)
  • Fasciculations → post-op myalgia
  • Bradycardia (especially 2nd dose; muscarinic)
  • Raised intraocular/intragastric/intracranial pressure
  • Prolonged apnoea (pseudocholinesterase deficiency)
  • Phase II block → prolonged paralysis
  • Histamine release (rare)
Non-Depol NMBs (general)
  • Residual block post-op → aspiration, hypoxia
  • Histamine release (benzylisoquinoliniums)
  • Hypotension (histamine-mediated)
  • Tachycardia (pancuronium — vagolytic)
  • Bronchospasm (atracurium, mivacurium)
  • Laudanosine toxicity (atracurium ICU) — seizures
  • Anaphylaxis (all — rare)
  • Prolonged block in organ failure
Baclofen
  • Withdrawal syndrome — seizures, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis
  • Sedation, dizziness, drowsiness
  • Weakness (dose-dependent)
  • Confusion, hallucinations (high dose/elderly)
  • Urinary retention
  • Nausea
  • Intrathecal pump failure — life-threatening withdrawal
  • Hypotonia (excessive dosing)
Tizanidine
  • Hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs; rare but fatal)
  • Sedation (significant)
  • Hypotension (orthostatic)
  • Dry mouth
  • Dizziness
  • CYP1A2 drug interaction → toxic levels
  • Rebound spasticity (abrupt withdrawal)
Cyclobenzaprine
  • Sedation (most common)
  • Dry mouth
  • Dizziness
  • Constipation
  • Arrhythmia (TCA structure — QT prolongation)
  • Serotonin syndrome with MAOIs/SSRIs
  • Urinary retention
  • Confusion (elderly)
Dantrolene
  • Hepatotoxicity — fatal (oral; monitor LFTs q3months)
  • Muscle weakness (dose-limiting)
  • Respiratory muscle weakness
  • Diarrhea, nausea
  • Dizziness, drowsiness
  • Phlebitis (IV — alkaline solution)
  • Pleural effusion (rare chronic use)
Benzodiazepines
  • Respiratory depression (+ opioids)
  • Dependence + withdrawal seizures
  • Sedation, cognitive impairment
  • Anterograde amnesia
  • Falls (elderly)
  • Paradoxical agitation
  • Tolerance to muscle relaxant effect
Botulinum Toxin
  • Local weakness (intended + adjacent muscles)
  • Dysphagia/aspiration (neck injections)
  • Ptosis (facial injections)
  • Pain at injection site
  • Systemic spread → generalised weakness
  • Anaphylaxis (rare)
  • Antibody formation (BoNT-B > BoNT-A)
  • Flu-like symptoms
Reversal Agents

Reversal of NMBs

Only relevant for neuromuscular blocking agents. Spasmolytics do not require pharmacological reversal.

Sugammadex
Modified γ-cyclodextrin — encapsulates amino-steroids

MechanismDirectly encapsulates NMB → lipophilic cavity traps drug → rapid dissociation from receptor → complete reversal

Dose (deep block, TOF = 0)4 mg/kg IV

Onset~3 min
AdvantageNo muscarinic side effects; works at any depth of block; complete reversal

📊
Train-of-Four (TOF) monitoring — use peripheral nerve stimulator at ulnar nerve. TOF count 0 = deep block. TOF ratio <0.9 = residual block (aspiration risk). Sugammadex works at any TOF. Neostigmine: only reliably effective at TOF count ≥2 (ideally ≥4 twitches). Always confirm recovery before extubation.
